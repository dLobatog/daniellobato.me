Lesson 03 · Neural networks · ~30 min + code

Backpropagation, by assigning blame

A click model predicted 0.90 for an impression that wasn't clicked. The loss is high, and the network has 9 parameters. How much is each one to blame, and can you find out without trying each one?

This lesson follows real numbers forward into the loss, then traces the blame back through every weight and every ReLU, one step at a time. Three kinds of quantity stay visually distinct: activations (values computed going forward), parameters (the weights being learned), and gradients (blame flowing backward).

1 · The question

Why not just nudge each weight?

A gradient answers a concrete question: if I move this one parameter a tiny bit, how much does the loss move?

You raise one weight by 0.01 and the loss goes up by 0.003. What is \(\partial L / \partial w\), approximately?

Change in loss over change in weight: \(0.003 / 0.01 = 0.3\). It's positive, so gradient descent lowers this weight. That's a finite-difference estimate, and it's how we'll check backprop later.

Nudging works. With a model of 1 billion parameters, how many forward passes would it take to get every gradient that way?

One nudge per parameter, so a billion passes per update. Backpropagation gets all of them from one forward pass plus one backward pass, costing roughly 2 to 3 forward passes in total. It does this by reusing work: the blame arriving at a node is computed once and shared by every weight behind it. The rest of this lesson is that reuse, made visible.

2 · Forward

Follow the numbers into the loss

Two features describe the impression: \(x_1 = 1.0\), the user has clicked this creator before, and \(x_2 = 0.6\), the item's normalized popularity. Two ReLU hidden units feed a sigmoid output. The impression was not clicked, \(y = 0\).

\[ z_j = w_{1j}x_1 + w_{2j}x_2 + b_j,\quad h_j = \max(0, z_j),\quad p = \sigma(v_1h_1 + v_2h_2 + c),\quad L = -\ln(1-p) \]

Hidden unit 2 computes \(z_2 = -0.5(1.0) - 1.0(0.6) + 0.2 = -0.9\). What does it pass on?

ReLU outputs \(\max(0, -0.9) = 0\). For this example, unit 2 is off. Keep that in mind: it decides what happens to its blame in the backward pass.

The output is \(p = \sigma(1.5 \cdot 1.40 + 0.8 \cdot 0 + 0.1) = \sigma(2.2) = 0.90\), and the loss is \(-\ln(1 - 0.90) = 2.305\). The model was confident and wrong.

3 · Backward

The blame game

Work backward from the loss. At each step, predict how much blame arrives, then watch it appear on the diagram. One rule does all the work, the chain rule: blame arriving at a quantity equals the blame at whatever it feeds, times how strongly it feeds it.

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial b}\cdot\frac{\partial b}{\partial a} \]

4 · Trust, but verify

Check backprop against nudging

Backprop and nudging must agree. Pick any parameter: the page nudges it by ±0.0001, reruns the forward pass, and compares the finite-difference slope with the backprop gradient.

Gradient check on all 9 parameters

This is how you debug a hand-written backward pass. A mismatch in one parameter points to the exact line that's wrong. In practice you compare relative error, \(|g_{\text{bp}} - g_{\text{fd}}| / \max(|g_{\text{bp}}|, |g_{\text{fd}}|)\), in float64. Around \(10^{-7}\) is fine, and \(10^{-2}\) is a bug. Avoid the kink of ReLU at exactly 0, where the slope isn't defined.

5 · A design decision

Why a sigmoid output pairs with cross-entropy

In section 3 the output blame was exactly \(p - y\). The sigmoid's own slope \(p(1-p)\) cancelled against the derivative of the log. That cancellation depends on the loss.

Swap the loss for squared error, \(L = (p - y)^2\). The model says \(p = 0.999\) and \(y = 0\). How big is the blame at the output logit?

\(\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = 2(p-y)\cdot p(1-p) = 2(0.999)(0.001) \approx 0.002\). The sigmoid is saturated, and its tiny slope multiplies the blame away. The model is as wrong as it can be, and it barely learns. With cross-entropy the blame is \(p - y = 0.999\).

Blame at the output logit for a negative example (y = 0)

The same thing happens inside a network. Every saturated sigmoid or tanh on the path multiplies the blame by a slope near 0. Stack enough of them and the early layers stop learning: that's the vanishing gradient. ReLU's slope is exactly 1 when it's on, which is a big part of why it replaced sigmoid in hidden layers. Its slope is exactly 0 when it's off, which is the "dead ReLU" risk you saw with unit 2.

6 · New cases, no hints

Apply it somewhere else

An embedding matrix is used twice: to embed input tokens and, tied, as the output projection. How does backprop compute its gradient?

When a quantity feeds the loss along several paths, the total derivative is the sum over paths (multivariable chain rule). Frameworks do this automatically by accumulating into .grad. That's also why forgetting zero_grad() silently sums gradients across steps.

You initialize both hidden units with identical weights. After many training steps, what are they?

Identical units compute identical activations, so they receive identical blame and identical updates, forever. The layer behaves like a single unit. Random initialization breaks the symmetry. Its scale (Xavier or He) keeps activations and gradients from shrinking or blowing up with depth.

A feature is encoded as 0 for every example in the batch, because it's missing. What gradient do the weights leaving that input get?

A weight's gradient is (blame at its output) × (its input), and the input is 0. Encoding "missing" as 0 makes it indistinguishable from a real zero, and the weights never learn anything about the missing case. Add an explicit missing indicator, or use a learned default embedding.

A 50-layer plain network (no residual connections) trains its last layers but its first layers barely move. The likeliest cause?

The gradient at layer 1 is a product of about 50 Jacobians. If their typical scale is below 1, it vanishes geometrically, and above 1 it explodes. Residual connections add an identity path: \(\partial(x + f(x))/\partial x = I + f'(x)\), so blame can flow back unattenuated. Normalization and careful initialization help too.

8 · Staff-level follow-ups

Answer out loud, then check

Why does training use reverse-mode differentiation (backprop) rather than forward mode?
  • Reverse mode computes the gradient of one scalar output with respect to all inputs in a single backward pass. Forward mode needs one pass per input direction.
  • Training has one scalar loss and billions of parameters, so reverse mode is the right trade: cost is about 2 to 3× a forward pass.
  • Forward mode wins when outputs outnumber inputs, for example Jacobian-vector products with few inputs.
What must be kept in memory for the backward pass, and how do you reduce it?
  • The activations each layer's weight gradient needs (its inputs), plus masks such as ReLU gates. This scales with batch size × sequence length × depth × width, and often dominates parameter memory.
  • Activation checkpointing stores only some layers' activations and recomputes the rest during the backward pass (about 30% more compute for large memory savings). Other levers: mixed precision, smaller micro-batches with gradient accumulation, and fused kernels.
How would you detect and fix dead ReLUs?
  • Detect: for each unit, the fraction of training examples where it's active. A unit active on 0% gets zero gradient forever.
  • Causes: a large update pushes the bias or weights so that the pre-activation is negative for all inputs. High learning rates are the usual trigger.
  • Fixes: lower learning rate or warmup, better initialization, LeakyReLU or GELU (nonzero slope for negatives), normalization before the activation.
Why do frameworks offer BCEWithLogits instead of sigmoid followed by BCE?
  • Numerical stability: \(\log(1 - \sigma(z))\) underflows to \(\log 0\) for large \(z\). The fused form computes \(\operatorname{softplus}(z) - yz\) stably.
  • The fused gradient is exactly \(\sigma(z) - y\), with no saturated slope to multiply through.
  • Same reason cross-entropy takes logits and uses log-softmax internally (lesson 1).
Explain vanishing and exploding gradients and three fixes.
  • Backprop multiplies local Jacobians layer by layer (or step by step in an RNN). A product of many factors with norms below or above 1 shrinks or grows geometrically.
  • Fixes: residual connections (identity path), normalization layers (LayerNorm or BatchNorm), variance-preserving initialization, gated recurrences (LSTM/GRU), and gradient clipping for explosions.
  • Diagnose by logging per-layer gradient norms, not just the loss.

Sources: Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, Learning representations by back-propagating errors (1986); Glorot & Bengio (2010) and He et al. (2015) on initialization; He et al., Deep Residual Learning (2015); Chen et al., Training Deep Nets with Sublinear Memory Cost (2016).
