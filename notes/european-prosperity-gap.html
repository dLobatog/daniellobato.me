The Swiss prosperity island

I spent two years working at CERN, living on the French side of the border and crossing into Geneva most mornings. The drive is fifteen minutes. The line on the map is invisible. The economics on either side of it are not.

Crossing from the Pays de Gex into the canton of Geneva is a bigger jump in measured prosperity than driving the entire length of Italy from Sicily to Lombardy. That always struck me as strange. Borders are supposed to be lines, not walls — and most European borders are just lines, with wealth fading smoothly across them. Switzerland is the exception. It sits in the middle of the continent like an island, abruptly and dramatically richer than everything it touches.

Read the map first

Look at the warm core in the middle. Basel-Stadt glows at €207,000 per head; Zug at €190,000. Now look immediately across the Rhine into Germany: Freiburg, at €36,600, is the pale yellow of an ordinary European region. Same river, same language, same Black Forest weather — and a 5.7× step in measured output per person. Flip on Highlight border pairs and the contrast becomes a ledger: thick red lines for the cliffs at the Swiss frontier, thin blue lines everywhere else, where the ratios sit near 1.0.

That asymmetry is the whole story. The Italian north–south divide, which Italians have argued about for 160 years, is roughly a 2× gap end to end. Crossing into Ticino from Lombardy is 2.8×. The border does more than the country.

Why Switzerland is an island

There is no single cause. There is a stack of mutually reinforcing ones, and the interesting thing is how they compound.

Federalism as a competitive market

Switzerland is 26 cantons that genuinely compete for residents and firms. A canton that overtaxes or overspends watches its tax base drive twenty minutes down the valley to a cheaper neighbour — Zug built an entire economy on exactly this. Tax competition is usually discussed as a bug; here it functions as a discipline. Government is pushed down to the smallest unit that can plausibly handle a task, and that unit has to justify itself against the one next door. The result is a public sector that is small, local, and unusually accountable.

A small state by European standards

Switch the map to Government size and the picture inverts: Switzerland turns pale while its neighbours darken. Swiss general government spends about 31% of GDP. France spends 56%, Italy and Austria around 51–56%, Germany 50%. That is not a moral verdict — France buys things with that money that the Swiss pay for privately — but it means a far larger share of Swiss output stays inside the private, market-priced economy that GDP measures well. Part of the cliff at the border is real productivity, and part is an accounting artifact of where the line between "public" and "private" is drawn.

A high-value-added economy

The Swiss don't make many things; they make expensive ones. Pharma and specialty chemicals cluster in Basel (which is why Basel-Stadt is an outlier even by Swiss standards), banking and commodity trading in Zürich and Geneva, precision instruments and machines across the Mittelland. These are sectors with enormous value added per worker. A country that specializes in the top of every value chain will show a high GDP per capita almost by construction.

The franc, and credibility

Switzerland kept its own currency and a central bank with a near-religious commitment to stability. The franc is a global safe-haven asset: when the world is frightened, money flows into it, pushing it up. A strong, trusted currency makes every Swiss nominal figure look large when converted to euros — and, more deeply, low and predictable inflation lowers the cost of capital and rewards long-horizon investment. Credibility is itself a productive asset.

Institutions that don't surprise you

Direct democracy, deep political stability, strong rule of law, light and predictable regulation, and a famously cooperative relationship between labour and capital. None of these is flashy. Together they lower the risk premium on doing business in Switzerland to about as close to zero as a country gets, and capital is exquisitely sensitive to risk. Money goes where it is treated predictably, and stays.

The gap wasn't always there

It's tempting to assume Switzerland was always rich, or — the opposite story I hear a lot — that it was dirt-poor until some clever policy flipped a switch. Neither is quite right, and the slider on the map is there to show why.

In 1850 Switzerland was a poor, mountainous, landlocked confederation whose biggest exports were cheese, embroidery, and mercenaries. In real terms it sat in the European middle of the pack — and behind Belgium and the Netherlands, which had industrialised first. Drag the slider back to the mid-19th century and the whole map goes pale: in absolute terms, almost everyone was poor, and the rich-poor spread between regions was small. There was no cliff to see.

What happened next wasn't one switch but a sequence. The 1848 federal constitution turned a loose alliance into a real state with one currency, one internal market, and credible institutions. Industrialisation followed — machines, watches, and the Basel chemical-dye houses that would become the global pharma giants. The Swiss National Bank arrived in 1907, anchoring the franc. And then the decisive, brutal accelerant: the two World Wars. Switzerland stayed neutral and physically intact while the capital stock of every neighbour was bombed flat. Watch the slider cross 1914–1945 and the island finally appears — not because Switzerland suddenly surged, but because everyone around it collapsed and then had to rebuild. The post-war decades — safe-haven franc, banking, pharma, precision manufacturing — locked the lead in.

So the honest version of your intuition is this: Switzerland's wealth is a long, compounding arc, but the discontinuity at the border — the cliff — is largely a 20th-century artifact, carved by war, monetary credibility, and the decision to integrate with Europe's markets without dissolving into its fiscal and monetary union.

A caveat I won't bury: only the 2022 layer is real per-region data. Every earlier year on the slider is modeled — I take each country's actual GDP-per-capita trajectory (Maddison Project historical estimates) and project today's regional pattern back along it. It's an illustration of the national arc, not a measurement of, say, Lombardy in 1913. The shape of the story is real; the per-region precision in old years is not.

Why every other border is smooth

The flat blue lines on the map are not an accident — they are the European Union working exactly as designed. The single market erased the economic meaning of the internal borders: goods, capital, services, and above all people move freely. When a worker can move to where wages are highest without a visa, and a firm can hire across the line without friction, prices and wages arbitrage toward each other. Add a shared currency across most of the bloc, decades of cohesion and structural funds aimed explicitly at convergence, and harmonized regulation, and you get exactly what the map shows: South Tyrol and Tyrol at 1.0×, Antwerp and North Brabant at 1.0×, the Basque Country and Aquitaine at 1.1×. Borders you can't feel.

Switzerland is outside that machine. It is bound to the EU by a patchwork of bilateral treaties rather than membership, keeps its own money and its own rules, and — crucially — does not let labour freely arbitrage the wage gap away. You can commute in, but you can't simply move in. So the gap doesn't close. It just sits there at the border, year after year, as a cliff instead of a slope.

What these numbers do and don't mean

Two honest caveats, because the cliff is partly a measurement effect and it would be dishonest to oversell it.

Prices, not just paychecks. These are nominal figures. Switzerland is also one of the most expensive places on earth — rent, groceries, a coffee. Adjust for purchasing power and the Swiss advantage shrinks a lot, though it does not vanish. A Geneva salary buys a Geneva life, not a Lisbon one.

Where the workers sleep. GDP is counted where output happens; population where people are registered. Roughly 350,000 cross-border commuters (frontaliers) pour into Geneva, Ticino, and Basel every day from France and Italy. Their output lands in the Swiss numerator while they live — and spend, and are counted — across the line. That mechanically inflates the per-capita figure of the border cantons and deflates the French and Italian regions feeding them. The very sharpest cliffs on this map are, in part, an artifact of a deeply integrated labour market drawn on top of a hard fiscal border.

Neither caveat undoes the picture. Switzerland is genuinely, structurally richer than its neighbours, for the stacked institutional reasons above. But the map is best read as a map of where the lines are drawn — fiscal, monetary, regulatory — as much as a map of who is "richer." The discontinuity is the point. Most of Europe spent seventy years sanding its borders down into slopes. Switzerland kept one as a wall, and you can see it from space.
